# Q&A for batch 2 workflows.

= git-objects
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: When Git behaves in a way you cannot explain, or you want to understand why branches are cheap and why identical files cost nothing.
q: What are the three object types?
a: A blob holds file bytes, a tree lists names and blobs like a folder, and a commit points to a tree and its parent.
q: What comes next?
a: git-reset-reflog and git-large-files make much more sense once you know that refs are just files holding a hash.

= git-large-files
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: A clone has become slow or the repository is far larger than the code, and you need to find out why.
q: Does ignoring a file shrink the repository?
a: No. Old versions stay in history. Only rewriting history (git filter-repo) or never committing the file removes them.
q: What comes next?
a: Use Git LFS for binaries you must version, and agree on a size limit with the team.

= git-notes-range-diff
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: A reviewer asked you to rebase and wants proof that nothing else changed, or you need to attach information to commits you cannot amend.
q: How do I read range-diff?
a: A line with = means the commit is identical after the rebase, ! means its patch changed, < or > means a commit was dropped or added.
q: What comes next?
a: Remember that notes are not pushed unless you push refs/notes/commits.

= git-patches
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: There is no shared server: an air-gapped network, an email-based project, or a quick hand-off to one colleague.
q: Patch, bundle or archive?
a: A patch carries a few commits, a bundle carries a whole repository with history, and an archive carries only files of one commit.
q: What comes next?
a: Use apply --check before am, so a failure leaves your repository untouched.

= git-force-push-recovery
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: Someone force-pushed over shared work, or you did and need the lost commits back.
q: Force or force-with-lease?
a: --force-with-lease refuses when the server has commits you have not seen, so you cannot silently erase a teammate's push.
q: What comes next?
a: Recover quickly from origin/main@{1} or a teammate's clone before the server cleans up unreachable commits.

= lnx-csv-report
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: A quick answer is needed from a CSV or log: totals per group, the largest value, an average. No spreadsheet is available on the server.
q: When is awk the wrong tool?
a: When fields contain quoted commas or newlines. Use a CSV-aware tool or Python for those files.
q: What comes next?
a: Save the one-liner as a script and run it from cron for a daily report.

= lnx-namespaces-cgroups
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: Understanding what a container really is, or debugging why a container hits a memory or CPU limit.
q: What is the difference between the two?
a: Namespaces limit what a process can see; cgroups limit how much it can use.
q: What comes next?
a: Read docker inspect and the pod resource limits with these two ideas in mind.

= lnx-audit-gpg
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: You must know who changed a sensitive file, or you must store a secret in a file safely.
q: Is a checksum a signature?
a: No. A checksum shows the file changed; only a signature shows who produced it.
q: What comes next?
a: Ship audit logs to a central place so an intruder cannot erase them.

= lnx-rsync-tar
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: Backups, migrating data between servers, deploying a folder, or packaging files for transfer.
q: What is the main danger?
a: --delete with a wrong path or a missing trailing slash. Always run with -n first and read the list.
q: What comes next?
a: Add a restore test, as in lnx-backup-restore.

= lnx-time-chrony
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: TLS errors, odd log times, failing token or Kerberos logins, or database clusters that disagree about order.
q: Why UTC on servers?
a: Logs from many machines and regions line up, and daylight saving never creates repeated or missing hours.
q: What comes next?
a: Monitor the clock offset and alert if it passes a few hundred milliseconds.

= lnx-kernel-sysctl
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: Preparing a machine for Kubernetes or routing, tuning network limits, or fixing "too many open files".
q: Why do changes disappear after reboot?
a: sysctl -w and ulimit change only the running system. Put the setting in /etc/sysctl.d, a systemd unit or limits.d.
q: What comes next?
a: Change one value at a time and record the old value.

= ans-pull-cron
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: Servers that scale automatically, laptops, or machines behind NAT that the control node cannot reach.
q: What do I give up compared to push?
a: A single central run. You need logging and monitoring to confirm that every host applied the latest commit.
q: What comes next?
a: Use --only-if-changed and a random delay so a thousand hosts do not pull at the same second.

= ans-dynamic-inventory
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: Cloud servers that come and go. A static host list is out of date the moment an autoscaling group changes.
q: How do I group hosts?
a: Use keyed_groups on tags, such as Role=web becoming role_web, so playbooks target by role, not by address.
q: What comes next?
a: Enable caching and give the plugin a read-only IAM role.

= ans-async-retry
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: A service needs time to start, a download is flaky, or a job takes longer than the SSH timeout.
q: until or wait_for?
a: wait_for suits ports and files. until with retries suits any module result, such as an HTTP status.
q: What comes next?
a: Prefer failed_when with a precise rule instead of ignore_errors.

= ans-molecule-test
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: A role is shared or critical and you want proof it works on a clean machine, and that a second run changes nothing.
q: What does molecule test do?
a: It creates an instance, converges, repeats to check idempotence, verifies, and destroys the instance.
q: What comes next?
a: Run it in CI on every pull request.

= ans-kubernetes-k8s
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: You already use Ansible and want cluster objects managed in the same repository and pipeline as the servers.
q: Ansible or kubectl and Helm?
a: Ansible is handy when Kubernetes is one part of a bigger run. For pure application delivery, Helm or GitOps tools are usually a better fit.
q: What comes next?
a: Check kubectl config current-context before every run, so you do not change the wrong cluster.

= net-ssh-forwarding
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: A database or admin page is private and you have SSH access to a host inside the network.
q: Which flag, in one line?
a: -L brings a remote service to your machine, -R sends your service to the server, -D makes a SOCKS proxy.
q: What comes next?
a: Close tunnels when finished, and use only systems you are authorised to reach.

= net-nmap-basics
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: Checking which ports your own servers really expose, or confirming that a firewall change worked.
q: What do open, closed and filtered mean?
a: Open means a program answers, closed means nothing listens, filtered means a firewall hides the answer.
q: What comes next?
a: Save scans and diff them after each change. Scan only with permission.

= net-wireguard
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: A small, fast VPN between two sites or from a laptop to a server, with little configuration.
q: What identifies a peer?
a: Its public key. AllowedIPs says which addresses may be sent through that peer.
q: What comes next?
a: Rotate keys on a schedule and never share a private key.

= net-iptables-nat
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: A Linux box must act as a router or gateway for a private network, or forward a public port to an internal server.
q: SNAT or DNAT?
a: MASQUERADE rewrites the source for outgoing traffic. DNAT rewrites the destination for port forwarding.
q: What comes next?
a: On new systems look at nftables, which replaces iptables.

= net-aws-peering-endpoints
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: Two VPCs, for example prod and shared services, need private connectivity, or private subnets need S3 without a NAT gateway.
q: Why does my peering not work?
a: Usual causes: only one route table was updated, a security group blocks it, or the CIDR ranges overlap.
q: What comes next?
a: For many VPCs, compare Transit Gateway.

= net-k8s-networkpolicy
q: In which situation do we use this?
a: Locking down a namespace so only the right pods can talk to each other, for example only the frontend reaching the database.
q: Where do people go wrong?
a: The network plugin must enforce policies, and after denying egress you must still allow DNS on port 53.
q: What comes next?
a: Test both the allowed and the denied path.
